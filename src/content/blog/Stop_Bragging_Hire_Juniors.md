Artificial intelligence made code cheap. That is not the same thing as engineering.

If you are a Chief Technology Officer, an engineering manager, or a technical leader, you are not paid to produce lines of code. You are paid to keep a complex system alive: reliable, understandable, and capable of change without breaking production.

That is why “we do not hire junior developers anymore” is not a flex. It is a warning sign.

A lot of teams are rationalizing the collapse in entry level hiring with a neat story: artificial intelligence writes better code, so we do not need beginners. But the real driver is more boring and more important: the hiring market changed. The era of cheap money and growth at all costs ended, hiring slowed, and suddenly there were far more entry level candidates than open roles. At the same time, demand tilted toward senior engineers because many organizations are trying to do more with fewer people.

Artificial intelligence amplified the bias. When raw code production gets faster, it becomes even easier to believe that “coding” is the job.

But engineering is maintenance. Engineering is understanding why the weird parts exist. Engineering is knowing which shortcut will become next quarter’s incident. That knowledge is specific to your company’s systems, your customers, your history, and your tradeoffs. And it lives in people.

Here is the contrarian move: hire junior developers anyway, but make it safe.

Treat junior hiring like resilience work. Ship with guardrails:

- Smaller changes that are easy to review and easy to roll back
- Reviews that teach, not just approve
- Runbooks that turn tribal knowledge into written knowledge
- Boring deployment checks that catch preventable mistakes

Those practices are not just “to protect seniors from juniors.” They make everyone better, and they make production less of a guessing game.

Junior developers are your continuity plan. They absorb institutional knowledge, then they become your next senior engineers. Skip that pipeline and your organization gets brittle. One key person leaves, and the team starts reverse engineering its own systems under pressure.

The twist is that artificial intelligence can actually strengthen the case for junior hiring. Many younger engineers adopt these tools quickly and help the rest of the team learn. If the tools reduce onboarding friction for basic tasks, your organization should invest the savings into mentorship and operational discipline, not into cutting the bottom rung off the ladder.
