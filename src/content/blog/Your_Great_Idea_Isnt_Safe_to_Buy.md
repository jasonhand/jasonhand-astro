Smart technical ideas are everywhere. Especially in data work. Someone has a cleaner way to model data, a better structure, a more “correct” approach, and a strong opinion about why every team should adopt it.

And then… nothing happens.

Here’s the uncomfortable truth I’ve learned as a practitioner and advisor: your idea probably isn’t failing because it is wrong. It is failing because nobody can safely buy it.

### The real cost shows up first
In real organizations, the costs hit immediately:

- Migration work that nobody planned for
- Integration headaches across systems
- Meetings, coordination, and timeline risk
- Politics and ownership debates

The benefit shows up later. Sometimes much later. And even then, the benefit is often invisible unless something breaks. That is a brutal market for any idea, no matter how technically elegant.

So when a community keeps repeating the same “correct” approach for years, and adoption still does not happen, blaming “lazy organizations” or “stupid users” is mostly a coping mechanism. The blocker is usually economics: limited attention, limited budget, limited capacity to execute, and a strong preference for decisions that feel reputationally safe.

### In a saturated world, ideas are cheap
With modern tools and artificial intelligence making it easier than ever to generate and ship new concepts, we are swimming in substitutes. The “price” of ideas is basically zero.

So what do people actually pay for?

They pay for outcomes.

They buy reduced uncertainty. Faster delivery. Lower operational risk. Fewer incidents. Clearer ownership. A measurable win they can defend to their boss and peers.

### Stop selling elegance. Sell an outcome.
If you want your data modeling or engineering idea to land, translate it into something a buyer can safely say yes to:

1. **Pick one measurable win.** Faster delivery, fewer incidents, clearer ownership, fewer broken reports.
2. **Scope it to a small pilot.** Make the commitment easy to approve and hard to regret.
3. **Name the risk you remove.** Put words (and ideally a number) on the uncertainty you are reducing.
4. **Speak in plain language.** Assume your audience does not share your vocabulary or your incentives.

This is not “selling” in the sleazy sense. It is outcome communication. It is empathy for the person who has to live with the decision after you move on to your next great idea.
